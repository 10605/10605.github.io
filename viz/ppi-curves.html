Classical vs PPI vs PPI++: interval width as n grows

As in Figure 3 of the paper: for each number of human labels n, draw many simulated datasets (n human-labeled examples plus N autorater-labeled ones), compute a 95% interval for P(H = 1) three ways, and average the widths.

Classical uses only the human labels (Clopper–Pearson). PPI is the Bayesian difference estimate of Eq 1, g = μ1 + μ2, with its interval from Monte Carlo integration. PPI++ is the power-tuned version of Eq 5:

g = 1⁄N Σj λ f(x̃j) + 1⁄n Σi (yi − λ f(xi)),    λ̂ = Cov(y, f) / ((1 + n/N) Var(f)) clipped to [0, 1]

Any λ gives the same expectation, E[y]. λ = 1 is PPI and λ = 0 ignores the autorater entirely, so PPI++ estimates the variance-minimizing λ from the labeled data and falls back toward the classical estimate when the autorater is weak. Try the presets: with a good autorater PPI and PPI++ both win, but with a coin-flip autorater PPI is worse than using the human labels alone.

Values under , more than three standard errors below 95% for this many datasets, are in red.

Data are generated as on page 1: H ~ Bernoulli(P(H=1)), and the autorater label A flips H with the given false-positive and false-negative rates. In each dataset, μ1 has posterior λ · Beta(m + ½, N − m + ½) and the rectifier mean has a Gaussian posterior with its standard error as the width (Student-t for n < 30); the interval comes from T Monte Carlo draws. Cov(y, f) is estimated from the labeled data and Var(f) from all the autorater labels, as in PPI++.

With a very accurate autorater and few human labels, PPI's coverage drops below 95% (try the “accurate” preset with 500 datasets per n and look at the coverage column). The reason is that many datasets then have no human–autorater disagreements at all: every rectifier value is 0, its sample standard deviation is 0, and the Gaussian posterior for μ2 collapses to a point, leaving only the (biased) autorater mean's uncertainty. With a 2% / 2% autorater, about a third of datasets at n = 50 look like this. The paper's curves likewise start at n = 50; a posterior for the rectifier that does not rely on its sample variance (e.g. a Dirichlet over its three values −1, 0, 1) would avoid the problem.
