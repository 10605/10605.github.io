The Lloyd–Max quantizer: J-means in one dimension

After the random rotation, TurboQuant replaces each coordinate t by one of J = 2b values c1 < … < cJ and stores only its index (b bits). The best values, the ones minimizing E[(t − cnearest)2], are found by Lloyd's algorithm, which is J-means (k-means) run on a 1-D distribution instead of a dataset:

Assign: each t goes to its nearest centroid. In 1-D every cell is an interval, and the boundaries are the midpoints (ck + ck+1)/2.   Update: each centroid moves to the mean of its cell, ck = ∫cell t f(t) dt / ∫cell f(t) dt; with a sample instead of a density this is the ordinary average of the points in the cell.

Because the rotation makes the density fd known in advance (it depends only on d), TurboQuant runs this once, offline, and uses the same codebook for every coordinate of every vector: no training data, and nothing to update as new vectors arrive.

For large d, the converged codebooks for fd, scaled by √d, are the optimal quantizers of a standard Gaussian, and d × (MSE per coordinate) is the expected ‖x − x̃‖2 for a unit vector. The TurboQuant paper reports ≈ 0.36, 0.117, 0.03, 0.009 for b = 1, 2, 3, 4, within a factor of about 2.7 of the information-theoretic lower bound 4−b.

The density is handled as 6000 weighted grid points, so the update integrals are sums. A cell with no mass (possible with a bad random start on a sample) keeps its centroid. Lloyd's algorithm only finds a local optimum in general; for log-concave densities like fd (d ≥ 3) and the Gaussian it is the global one, but try a random start on “two bumps”.

S. Lloyd, Least squares quantization in PCM (1957, published 1982); J. Max, Quantizing for minimum distortion (1960).
