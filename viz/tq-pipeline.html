TurboQuant end to end

Putting the pieces together on many key vectors x from a KV cache, with b bits per coordinate. TurboQuantmse rotates, quantizes every coordinate with the b-bit Lloyd–Max codebook, and rotates back. It minimizes ‖x − x̃‖2, but its inner products ⟨y, x̃⟩ are biased toward zero. TurboQuantprod spends b − 1 bits on TurboQuantmse and the last bit on QJL applied to the residual r = x − x̃mse, which makes ⟨y, x̃⟩ an unbiased estimate of ⟨y, x⟩ for every query y.

Over many keys and queries

Random key/query pairs (keys with outlier channels, normalized to unit length; queries at random angles to them). Each dot is one pair, quantized with its own random Π and S: TurboQuant's guarantees are expectations over that randomness. (In a real cache one Π and S are shared, and across many different keys the errors average out the same way; our synthetic keys are all dominated by the same outlier channels, so with one shared draw they would mostly see the same luck, good or bad.)

Slope: least-squares fit of the estimate against the true ⟨y, x⟩ (1 = unbiased). Paper's upper bounds on E‖x − x̃‖² for TurboQuantmse: √3π/2 · 4−b ≈ 0.68, 0.17, 0.043, 0.011, with actual values ≈ 0.36, 0.117, 0.03, 0.009. For TurboQuantprod at b = 1 the whole budget goes to QJL.

Inner-product residuals ⟨y, x̃⟩ − ⟨y, x⟩

The true inner products are spread symmetrically around 0, so shrinkage toward zero cancels in the plain mean; the "toward 0" column multiplies each residual by sign⟨y, x⟩, so negative values mean the estimates are pulled toward zero (bias). TurboQuantmse is biased but has lower variance; TurboQuantprod is unbiased but has higher variance.

Which variant is used in practice?

Mostly TurboQuantmse. The paper's downstream experiments (needle-in-a-haystack, LongBench, near-neighbor search) use TurboQuantprod but never compare it with TurboQuantmse at the same bit budget, and the paper's own Fig. 2a shows prod winning on inner-product error only at low bit widths. Practitioners have generally dropped the QJL stage: vLLM's TurboQuant backend omits it because it was found to "hurt attention quality by amplifying variance through softmax", and llama.cpp implementers report the same. A perplexity comparison on Qwen2.5-7B reproduced in HyperQuant finds the MSE variant better at 2, 3 and 4 bits. Unbiasedness removes the shrinkage of attention scores, but the extra variance it costs (the wider histogram above) matters more.

Real attention heads use d = 64 to 128; the guarantees improve as d grows. QJL here uses m = d rows, so S is d×d. The paper also handles outlier channels by giving them more bits (e.g. 2.5 or 3.5 bits on average); that refinement is not shown.
