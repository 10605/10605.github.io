Successive halving

Choosing the winner of a science fair: each project (a hyperparameter configuration) has an unknown true quality, and each judgment (a training run or evaluation) gives a noisy score. Uniform allocation spreads the budget of B judgments evenly, so most of it goes to projects that are clearly bad, and the best few can stay statistically indistinguishable. Successive halving works in rounds: judge every surviving project equally, drop the worse half, and repeat, so each round spends the same share of the budget on half as many projects. Bars show judgments so far; dots show each project's average score with a 95% interval.

Repeat many times

Rerun both methods on these projects with fresh judgments each time.

Each judgment is the project's true quality plus Gaussian noise with standard deviation σ (higher scores are better). Intervals are mean ± 1.96·σ/√m for m judgments. Both methods draw from the same sequence of judgments for each project, so differences come from how the budget is allocated, not from luck. Uniform allocation is shown spending its budget at the same rate as successive halving, round by round. In each round, successive halving gives every surviving project rk more judgments and ranks projects by the average of all their judgments so far.

Science-fair framing after Kevin Jamieson's slides; algorithm from Li, Jamieson, DeSalvo, Rostamizadeh and Talwalkar, Hyperband: A Novel Bandit-Based Approach to Hyperparameter Optimization (JMLR 2018), which runs successive halving several times with different n.
